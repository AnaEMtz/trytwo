Panel of Experts

Small teams of independent specialists created by the Security Council to gather evidence on sanctions implementation and violations.

What is a Panel of Experts?

Panels (also called Groups of Experts or Monitoring Teams, depending on the regime) assist a sanctions committee by collecting, examining and analysing information about implementation and non-compliance.

  • Established by a Security Council resolution with a defined mandate
  • Experts are appointed by the Secretary-General and serve in an individual capacity
  • Typical expertise: arms and ordnance, customs and trade, finance, maritime and aviation, regional affairs
  • Mandates are time-limited and must be renewed by the Council, often annually

What do Panels do?

Their work typically falls into three connected tasks.

1

Investigate

Gather evidence through field visits, interviews, open-source research, satellite imagery, trade and shipping data, and information shared by governments and companies.

2

Report

Submit periodic reports to the sanctions committee, typically a mid-term update and a final report, documenting cases of non-compliance.

3

Recommend

Propose new designations, improvements to the sanctions regime and practical steps for states and industry to strengthen implementation.

How a report comes together

A simplified view of the typical process.

Step 1

Mandate and planning

The Council renews or sets the Panel’s mandate; experts agree a work plan around priority issues.

Step 2

Collection and field work

Experts seek information from governments, companies, ports, banks and open sources, and inspect seized or documented items.

Step 3

Analysis and verification

Findings are cross-checked against multiple sources and, where possible, shared with the states or companies concerned for comment.

Step 4

Submission to the committee

The report goes to the sanctions committee and then the Security Council, accompanied by recommendations.

Step 5

Publication and follow-up

Reports are generally made public, informing governments, regulators, banks and the shipping industry.

Challenges and the road ahead

Panels are only as effective as the access, cooperation and political backing they receive.

Access and cooperation. Experts often depend on states and companies to share data and grant access, which is not always forthcoming.
Political dynamics. Because mandates need Council approval, disagreements among members can end a Panel’s work.
Resources and safety. Panels are small, work under tight budgets and sometimes face pressure or intimidation.
Alternative mechanisms. After the DPRK Panel ended, 11 states created the Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team in 2024 to continue public reporting outside the UN framework.
